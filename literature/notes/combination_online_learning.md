## 1. Wintenberger (2017) — Optimal learning with Bernstein Online Aggregation
**File:** `literature/pdf/wintenberger2017_boa.pdf`
**Citation as printed:** Olivier Wintenberger, "Optimal learning with Bernstein Online Aggregation", Sorbonne Universités UPMC Paris 06 & University of Copenhagen; arXiv:1404.1356v5 [stat.ML], 13 Sep 2016. (Published in Machine Learning 106:119–141, 2017.)

**Core contribution.** Introduces BOA, a recursive exponential-weights procedure whose weights include a *second-order refinement*: π_{j,t} ∝ exp(−η ℓ_{j,t}(1+η ℓ_{j,t})) π_{j,t−1}, where ℓ_{j,t} is the (linearized) instantaneous regret ℓ(Y_t, f_{j,t−1}) − E_{π_{t−1}}[ℓ(Y_t, f_{J,t−1})]. BOA is proved to be **the first online algorithm whose batch version achieves the optimal fast rate log(M)/n in deviation** for the model-selection (MS) aggregation problem in the bounded i.i.d. setting with square-loss-like (Lipschitz + strongly convex) losses. Classical EWA/progressive-mixture rules attain log(M)/n only *in expectation* and are provably suboptimal in deviation (Audibert 2009); the second-order term is what fixes this. The key technical tools are a new *empirical* Bernstein inequality for martingales (Thm 1.1: E[exp(M_n − [M]_n)] ≤ 1 if ΔM_t ≥ −1/2 a.s.) and a stochastic conversion that bounds the cumulative predictive risk in *any* stochastic environment (not just i.i.d.).

**Theoretical guarantees (precise).**
- *Second-order regret bound* (Thm 3.1): if η max_{t,j}|ℓ_{j,t}| ≤ 1/2, then E_π̂[Err_{n+1}(f_J)] ≤ inf_π { E_π[Err_{n+1}(f_J) + η Σ_{t=1}^{n+1} ℓ²_{J,t}] + K(π,π₀)/η }; with the gradient trick the same holds against the best *convex combination* f_π.
- *Multiple learning rates* (Thm 3.2): per-expert rates η_j, same form with log(π_j/π_{j,0}) adjusted; with optimally tuned η_j = sqrt(log M / Σ_t ℓ²_{j,t}), Err(f̂) ≤ min_π {Err(f_π) + 2 sqrt(log M) E_π[sqrt(Σ_t ℓ²_{J,t})]}.
- *Fully adaptive BOA* (Fig. 2 + Thm 3.3): online-calibrated per-expert rates η_{j,t} = min(1/E_j, sqrt(log(π_{j,0}^{-1}) / Σ_{s≤t} ℓ²_{j,s})) with per-expert range estimates E_{j,t} via a per-expert doubling trick; second-order regret bound with an extra additive term B_{n,E} = log(1 + ½log n + log E + c log 2), i.e. adaptivity costs a **log log(n)** factor.
- *Stochastic/batch results*: Problem (C) (convex aggregation): rate sqrt(log(M)/n) in deviation in any stochastic environment. Problem (MS): with probability 1 − e^{−x}, R̄(f̄) ≤ R_{n+1}(f̂)/(n+1) ≤ min_j R(f_j) + (2 log M + 3x)/(η(n+1)) for η^{-1} ≳ C_b²/C_ℓ (C_b-Lipschitz, C_ℓ-strongly convex loss, uniform prior) — the optimal fast rate; adaptive BOA achieves it up to log log(n).

**Datasets/results.** Pure theory paper; no empirical application, no electricity data.

**Relevance hooks for the project:** this is the canonical reference for why BOA rather than EWA: optimality *in deviation* (robustness of a single run, not just on average), second-order variance term stabilizes weights, multiple per-expert learning rates are built in and fully adaptive (no tuning). Nothing here on smoothing or forgetting.

## 2. Berrisch & Ziel (2021) — CRPS Learning
**File:** `literature/pdf/berrisch_ziel2021_crps_learning.pdf`
**Citation as printed:** Jonathan Berrisch, Florian Ziel (University of Duisburg-Essen), "CRPS Learning", arXiv:2102.00968v3 [stat.ML], 19 Nov 2021; preprint submitted to Journal of Econometrics (status: accepted). (Published: J. Econometrics 237(2):105221, 2023.)

**Core contribution.** Probabilistic forecast combination that minimizes the CRPS via **pointwise aggregation across quantiles** (horizontal aggregation / Vincentization): weights w_{t,k}(p) vary over time *and* across the probability level p, because experts differ in where in the distribution they are good (center vs tails). CRPS is written as 2∫₀¹ QL_p(F^{-1}(p),y)dp, so combination reduces to per-quantile quantile-loss aggregation on a grid P (percentiles 1%…99%). They prove pointwise learning weakly dominates constant-weight CRPS learning (Prop. 1: R̄^QL_{t,min} ≤ R̂^CRPS_{t,min}, strict unless optimal weight functions are constant), propose B-spline/P-spline representations of the weight functions, and give a **fully adaptive gradient-based BOA (BOAG)** algorithm (Algorithm 1 "P-Smoothed CRPS Bernstein Online Aggregation") with optimality guarantees.

**Theoretical guarantees (precise).** Optimality definitions (from Wintenberger 2017): selection optimal if (R̃_t − R̂_{t,min})/t = O(log K / t); convex-aggregation optimal if (R̃_t − R̂_{t,conv})/t = O(sqrt(log K / t)).
- EWA satisfies selection optimality only for exp-concave losses; **the quantile loss is convex but NOT exp-concave**, so EWA(G) has no guarantee — this motivates BOA.
- Fully adaptive BOAG (Gaillard & Wintenberger 2018, Thm 2.1): P((R̃_t − R̂_{t,min})/t ≤ C((log K + log log(Gt) + x)/(αt))^{1/(2−β)}) ≥ 1 − 2e^{−x} for convex G-Lipschitz weak exp-concave losses; β = 1 gives the fast O(log K/t) rate up to log log(t).
- Wintenberger Thm 4.3: (R̃_t − R̂_{t,conv})/t ≤ C log log(t)(sqrt(log K/t) + (log K + x)/t) w.p. ≥ 1 − e^{−x} for convex losses with convergent variance V_{t,k}/t → V_k < ∞.
- **Proposition 2:** the second subderivative of the quantile risk Q_p(x) = E[QL_p(x,Y)] equals the density f; if f ≥ γ > 0 on its support, Q_p is γ-strongly convex (⇒ weak exp-concave with β = 1).
- **Theorem 1:** BOAG applied pointwise on QL_p for all p achieves convex-aggregation optimality with minimal CRPS (R̂_{t,conv} = R̄^QL_{t,conv}) if V < ∞; and if Y_t|F_{t−1} has pdf f_t ≥ γ > 0, also selection optimality with β = 1. In words: **fast O(t) convergence to the best expert and O(√t) to the best convex combination, up to log log t** — pointwise, hence with minimal CRPS over the larger pointwise-weight space.

**Smoothing, forgetting, extensions (Section 5).**
- *Penalized P-spline smoothing of the weight functions across quantiles* after each update: w_t(P) = B(B'B + λ(α D₁'D₁ + (1−α) D₂'D₂))^{-1} B' φ(P) β_t (eq. 42). They deliberately use α > 0 (recommend α = 0.5) so λ→∞ shrinks toward **constant** weights (standard CRPS learning), not toward a linear weight-probability relationship — "no clear argument in CRPS learning supports shrinkage towards a linear relationship". λ chosen online from an exponential grid by past cumulative performance.
- *Forgetting* (eq. 43): R_{t,k}(ξ) = (1−ξ)R_{t−1,k} + ℓ(F̃_t,Y_t) − ℓ(F̂_{t,k},Y_t); ξ = 0 means no forgetting; "in practice optimal ξ values are usually close to 0"; must be applied to all hidden state variables (R, E, V). Exponential forgetting destroys asymptotic guarantees (exponential decays too fast; polynomial discounts would preserve them) but dominates applied work.
- Shrinkage operators: fixed share F(x;φ)=φ/K+(1−φ)x, soft- and hard-thresholding.
- Quantile crossing: recommend always sorting the combined quantiles (never hurts QL, per Chernozhukov et al. 2010).
- Implementation: open-source R package **profoc** (BOA, EWA, ML-Poly, batch QR, B/P-spline smoothing); online hyperparameter selection by cumulative past loss over a parameter grid.

**Simulations (1000 reps, K=2 Gaussian experts, grid = percentiles).**
- Study 1 (fixed optimal weights varying over p): Pointwise beats both constant variants given enough data; **both smoothing methods improve on Pointwise at every sample size (T=32,128,512); P-Smooth best overall**; P-Constant (per-quantile learning rates then constant weights) beats B-Constant (single learning rate) for small T.
- Study 2: same DGP, algorithms compared — **BOAG converges fastest; lower distance-to-optimum than EWAG and ML-PolyG at all sample sizes**.
- Study 3 (changing optimal weights): mean CRPS — Pointwise no-forget 0.2956, P-Smooth no-forget 0.2953, Pointwise+forget 0.2943, **P-Smooth+forget 0.2930** (best; forget grid 2^x, chosen online).

**Application: EUA carbon prices** (not electricity): daily month-ahead EUA futures, Phase III Jan 2013–Dec 2020, 2092 obs, rolling window 250, T=1841 out-of-sample days; 5 experts (ETS-ANN, QuantReg, ARMA(1,0,1)-GARCH(1,1), ARIMA(0,1,0)-I-EGARCH, I-GARCH with Student-t). Table 2 (CRPS difference to Naive uniform combination, ×10⁻⁴, negative = better; DM p-values vs Naive):
- Experts: ETS +2.101, QuantReg +1.358, ARMA-GARCH +0.520, I-EGARCH +0.511, I-GARCHt −0.037 — only one expert beats the naive average.
- BOAG: Pointwise −0.170, B-Constant −0.118, P-Constant −0.138 (p=.020), B-Smooth −0.173, **P-Smooth −0.182 (best, p=.039)**.
- EWAG P-Smooth −0.107; ML-PolyG P-Smooth −0.160; BMA ≈ +0.04 (no better than naive); batch QR (QRlin/QRconv) poor and expensive.
- Findings: **BOAG best among all learners; P-Smooth (quantile smoothing) improves Pointwise for all three online learners** (B-Smooth does not); online methods ≈ best expert in the lower tail and ≈ naive in the upper tail, adapting to where each expert is good. In the first year the best single expert (I-GARCHt) still led; learners caught up and overtook thereafter — learners need time to stabilize.

## 3. Berrisch & Ziel (2024) — Multivariate probabilistic CRPS learning (day-ahead prices)
**File:** `literature/pdf/berrisch_ziel2024_multivariate_crps.pdf`
**Citation as printed:** Jonathan Berrisch, Florian Ziel, "Multivariate Probabilistic CRPS Learning with an Application to Day-Ahead Electricity Prices", arXiv:2303.10019v3 [stat.ML], 6 Feb 2024; preprint submitted to International Journal of Forecasting (status: accepted). (Published IJoF 41(4), 2025.)

**Core contribution.** Extends CRPS learning to **multivariate** distributional forecasts: weights w_{t,k}(d,p) vary over time, over quantiles p, **and over marginals d (= the 24 hours of the day)**, with dependencies between neighboring hours and quantiles captured by smoothing. Two smoothing devices: (i) dimension reduction of the D×P regret via bivariate basis matrices (B^mv, B^pr) — bridges pointwise and constant weights; (ii) **penalized P-spline smoothing in BOTH dimensions** (eq. 15–16: separate penalties λ^pr across probabilities and λ^mv across marginals/hours; hat matrices H^mv, H^pr precomputed). Algorithm 2 = "Smoothed CRPS Bernstein Online Aggregation" with forgetting θ, fixed share φ, thresholding κ, ν, learning-rate scaling γ, and non-equidistant knot placement (beta-distributed knots) — 15 tunable hyperparameters, tuned by three strategies: Bayesian fix (mlrMBO on burn-in), Sampling Online (online selection over 2500 random parameter sets by past cumulative CRPS), Bayesian Online (mix). This is the **one paper in the set that smooths weights across HOURS** (marginals), not just across quantiles. Fast C++ implementation in R package profoc.

**Theory.** Inherits the univariate guarantees (BOA yields "optimal asymptotic learning properties", i.e., convergence to best expert and best convex combination per Wintenberger 2017 / Berrisch & Ziel 2021); no new theorems — extensions (forget, shrinkage) are motivated empirically; exponential forgetting again noted to void asymptotic guarantees.

**Data/design.** German day-ahead hourly electricity prices; probabilistic forecasts of Marcjasz et al. (2023): **8 distributional deep-learning experts (4 Gaussian "Norm1–4", 4 Johnson's SU "JSU1–4" DDNNs)**, Dec 27 2018 – Dec 31 2020, 736 days × 24 hours × 99 percentiles; first 182 days burn-in (554 evaluation days); DM test on daily 24-h CRPS L1 norms vs Naive.

**Headline numbers (Table 2, mean CRPS; lower better).** Experts: JSU4 1.374 (best single), JSU2 1.444, Norm1 1.414 … all worse than **Naive (uniform) 1.295**. Combinations (full expert set):
- BOA: Constant 1.2933, Pointwise 1.2936, FTL 1.3752, B-Constant-Pr 1.2936, B-Constant-Mv 1.2918; Forget (Bayesian Online) 1.2855**; **Smooth.Forget Bayesian Online 1.2845** / Sampling Online 1.2845*** (best overall).
- ML-Poly best 1.2861–1.2873; EWA best 1.2864 — **BOA beats ML-Poly and EWA across specifications**.
- Magnitudes: best combination vs best single expert: 1.2845 vs 1.374 ≈ **6.5% CRPS improvement**; vs naive uniform: 1.295 → 1.2845 ≈ **0.8% improvement, statistically significant (DM)**. Subset runs (JSU-only, Norm-only in Appendix B.1) show same pattern.
- **Decomposition of gains: "forgetting contributes most of the observed improvement"** (Forget alone 1.2855 vs Smooth alone 1.2918 vs both 1.2845); dynamic hyperparameter tuning (Bayesian/Sampling Online) > static Bayesian fix.
- Weight analysis (Figs 2, 5–7): optimized pointwise weights are extremely noisy; smoothed weights are stable; weights vary with time, hour, and quantile; **"the weights show less smoothing across hours than across quantiles"** (chosen λ^pr > λ^mv) — i.e., the data wants *some but milder* smoothing in the hour dimension. Structural breaks found around June 2019 and March 2020 (COVID). Quantile crossing occurred in ≥1 marginal on 67 of 554 days (sorting applied). Compute: best scheme ≈ 14 min for the whole task (i5-12600K).

## 4. Devaine, Gaillard, Goude & Stoltz (2013) — specialized (sleeping) experts, electricity load
**File:** `literature/pdf/devaine2013_sleeping_experts_load.pdf`
**Citation as printed:** Marie Devaine, Pierre Gaillard, Yannig Goude, Gilles Stoltz, "Forecasting electricity consumption by aggregating specialized experts — A review of the sequential aggregation of specialized experts, with an application to Slovakian and French country-wide one-day-ahead (half-)hourly predictions", arXiv:1207.1965v1, 9 Jul 2012. (Published: Machine Learning 90(2):231–260, 2013.)

**Core contribution.** Framework of prediction with *specialized/sleeping* experts (only a subset E_t active each round); three aggregation-rule families with regret bounds: exponentially weighted average E_η restricted to active experts, the specialist rule S_η (general Hoeffding-based analysis improving Freund et al. 1997), and a fixed-share rule F_{η,α} adapted to sleeping experts (tracking compound experts with few shifts). Gradient-trick versions compete with the best convex combination. Also a practical fully-sequential meta-rule (Gerchinovitz/Mallet) that tunes η (and α) online by picking at each t the grid parameter with smallest past cumulative loss — because theoretically optimal η* perform terribly in practice.

**Theoretical guarantees.** Convex, bounded losses, L = bound on loss range:
- Thm 1 (E_η): max_j R_T ≤ ln N/η + (η/2)L²T; optimal η* gives L·sqrt(2T ln N).
- Thm 2 (S_η): ln N/η + (η/8)L²T → L·sqrt((T/2) ln N).
- Cor 1 (gradient versions, vs best convex weights q): ln N/η + 2ηG²T (E^grad) and ln N/η + (η/2)G²T (S^grad).
- Thm 3 (fixed share F_{η,α}, vs compound experts with ≤ m shifts): (m+1)/η · ln N + (1/η) ln[1/(α^m(1−α)^{T−m−1})] + (η/8)L²T; tuned: L·sqrt((T/2)((m+1)ln N + (T−1)H(m/(T−1)))) = o(T) when m = o(T). Cor 2: gradient version same with (η/2)G²T.
- No guarantee offered for the empirical meta-rule tuning.

**Datasets & results (load, not price; square loss, RMSE).**
- *Slovakian* hourly consumption, 35 black-box experts, Jan 1 2005–Dec 31 2007 (1095 days); **data split into 24 per-hour subsets, one aggregation problem per hour of day** (results reported for 11:00–12:00). Oracles: uniform sequential rule U 31.1 MW; uniform convex vector 30.7; **best single expert 30.4; best convex weight vector 29.2**; best compound experts: m=10: 32.1, m=50: 23.1, m=200: 15.2; prescient 9.4. Results with best constant parameters in hindsight: E_η 30.5; **E_η^grad and S_η^grad 28.2** (≈3% better than the best-convex oracle); **fixed-share F_{η,α} 27.0, F^grad_{η,α} 27.2**. Online tuning via grids preserves performance: E^grad 28.2–28.4; F 27.8, F^grad 28.5.
- *French* EDF operational data: 24 experts (15 parametric "Eventail", 8 GAM, 1 functional "similarity"), half-hourly, validation Sep 1 2007–Aug 31 2008 (320 days kept, T = 15 360), with an **operational constraint**: at 12:00 produce forecasts for the next 48 half-hours (rules W, T, G access base-rule weights only at t_k = 48k+1 and adapt via renormalization/share updates — their way of handling the 24h-ahead structure; no smoothing of weights across hours, but share updates propagate weights across the delivery day). Oracles: U 0.724 GW; uniform convex 0.748; **best single expert 0.782; best convex 0.683**; best compound m=50: 0.534, m=100: 0.474. Results (best constant params): **W_η^grad 0.629** (uniform prior; fair prior 0.633) — ≈5% better than best-convex oracle and ≈20% better than the best single expert; basic (non-gradient) versions only ≈ best-convex (0.684–0.718). **Fixed-share-type G_{η,α}^grad: 0.598** (≈12% below best convex, ≈23.5% below best single expert). Fully online tuning (adaptive grids): W^grad 0.640–0.644, G 0.658, **G^grad 0.623** — "fully sequential character comes at limited cost".
- Robustness (Sec 5.6): aggregation improves not just average RMSE but the whole residual distribution — half-hourly RMSE and 50/75/90% quantiles of |residuals| of the meta-rules are below best-expert and best-convex benchmarks for most half-hours (Figs 10–11); large errors occur less frequently.
- Practical notes highly relevant to the project: theoretically optimal learning rates (η* ≈ 10⁻⁸/10⁻⁶) are useless in practice; empirical online tuning on a grid is what works; gradient versions matter a lot; weights do not converge — they keep adapting (Fig 5).

## 5. Nowotarski & Weron (2016) — "To combine or not to combine?" (file named hubicka2016)
**File:** `literature/pdf/hubicka2016_combine_or_not_wp.pdf`
**Citation as printed:** Jakub Nowotarski, Rafał Weron (Dept. of Operations Research, Wrocław University of Technology), "To combine or not to combine? Recent trends in electricity price forecasting", HSC Research Report HSC/16/01, preprint submitted to ARGO Review, January 18, 2016.

**Core contribution.** Short empirical position paper arguing that combining forecasts (averaging/aggregation/committee machines) is worth it in EPF for both point and probabilistic forecasts. Uses GEFCom2014 Price Track data and 4 standard time-series models; compares three point-combination schemes (SIMPLE arithmetic mean, constrained least squares CLS with nonnegative weights summing to 1, LAD regression) and **Quantile Regression Averaging (QRA)** for probabilistic forecasts (quantile regression on the vector of individual point forecasts; notes QRA won the top two places at GEFCom2014; FQRA/PCA variant for many experts).

**Theory.** None (empirical note). Key argument: the main advantage of combining is not that combinations beat the best individual ex post — "this may not always be the case" — but that **it is less risky to combine than to select ex ante a single model**.

**Data/design.** GEFCom2014 Price Track: hourly zonal prices + day-ahead zonal load forecasts, Dec 19 2011–Dec 18 2013. Split: 182 days calibration of individual models; 183 days estimation of combination weights; **52-week out-of-sample test Dec 18 2012–Dec 16 2013**; daily re-estimation, rolling. Individual models: AR, ARX (zonal load as exogenous), TAR, TARX (threshold variants); all on log-prices; weekly-weighted MAE (WMAE) per week; Winkler scores and Christoffersen conditional-coverage tests for 50%/90% PIs, **tests run separately for each of the 24 hours**.

**Headline numbers.**
- Point (Table 1, mean WMAE over 52 weeks): **SIMPLE 11.26 (best), CLS 11.33, LAD 11.48** vs individuals AR 11.71, TAR 12.00, ARX 11.68, TARX 11.64. Mean deviation from best model in each week: SIMPLE 0.63, CLS 0.69, LAD 0.84 vs 1.00–1.37 for individuals. **All three combination schemes beat every individual model** (gain of SIMPLE vs best individual TARX ≈ 3.3% WMAE); SIMPLE is the most accurate and robust.
- Probabilistic (Table 2, Winkler scores): 50% PI: **QRA 24.13** vs ARX 24.63, TARX 24.53; 90% PI: **QRA 53.07** vs ARX 56.60, TARX 54.16. Conditional-coverage LR tests by hour: for 50% PIs QRA passes in 16 of 24 hours at 5% (20 at 1%) vs ARX 3 (7) and TARX 2 (3); for 90% PIs at the 1% level QRA passes 7 hours, TARX 1, ARX 0.
- Conclusions: combination quality "depends on the quality of the building blocks"; if one individual model dominates continuously there may be no advantage in combining; the case for combining is risk reduction vs ex-ante selection.

## 6. El Mahtout & Ziel (2026) — Bridging Linear Models, Neural Networks and Online Learning
**File:** `literature/pdf/bridging_linear_nn_online2026.pdf`
**Citation as printed:** Btissame El Mahtout (Univ. Duisburg-Essen & TU Dortmund), Florian Ziel (Univ. Duisburg-Essen), "Electricity Price Forecasting: Bridging Linear Models, Neural Networks and Online Learning", arXiv:2601.02856v4 [cs.LG], 4 Sep 2026.

**What it does.** (i) Multivariate (24-output) hybrid architecture: a linear skip connection from input to output (RLin = per-hour regressors only; FLin = all 24 hours' regressors, 175 regressors/hour) plus a one-hidden-layer Leaky-ReLU MLP ("MLP with RLin/FLin", wide-&-deep style after Cheng et al. 2016); variants MLP (no skip), RLin with OLS, MLP with RLin and OLS (OLS-coefficient initialization of the skip). Regressors: price lags (d−1 all hours, d−2, d−7, P_{d−1,23}), day-ahead load/solar/wind-on/offshore forecasts, commodity prices (gas, coal, oil, EUA at d−2), weekday dummies. (ii) **Novel "partial online learning"**: warm-start weights from the previous window + **window-specific hyperparameters** — initial window (D_init obs, 60 epochs, own learning rate) then rolling update windows of D_up most recent observations (10 epochs, smaller LR); D_init, D_up, LRs, neurons, L2/L1 penalties tuned by TPE/Optuna (500 trials, MAE objective). (iii) **First application of fully adaptive BOA (Wintenberger 2017, implementation as in Berrisch & Ziel 2023) to electricity price forecasting** (previously only load: Vilmarest et al. 2023; Hirsch et al. 2024; Himych et al. 2024). BOA is applied with **per-hour weights w_{k,d,h}** (separate weight per expert k and hour h, updated daily with the full adaptive recursion incl. second-order refinement, E/V range and variance estimators, gradient trick; initial weights 1/K; no forgetting/smoothing mentioned — plain fully adaptive BOA, run independently per hour). Ensembles: per model class, forward stepwise selection of 10 of the 500 TPE candidate models by validation-MAE of the BOA ensemble ("[Class] (BOA)"); plus "BOA all" pooling across classes.

**Data/design.** ENTSO-E hourly day-ahead prices, German–Luxembourg and Spain, 2018-10-01 to 2025-01-14 (2298 days, 55 153 h); test = last 2 years (2023-01-16–2025-01-14, 730 days), validation = 2 prior years; daily recalibration everywhere. Benchmarks: **LEAR** and **DNN** from Lago et al. 2021 epftoolbox (2-year windows; commodity prices added for fairness) and **GAM Online** (mgcv with Wood et al. 2015 online QR-update, per hour, tuned the same way). Metrics RMSE/MAE/rMAE (vs similar-day naive); DM tests (Harvey small-sample correction, NW variance, daily aggregated L1 and L2 losses).

**Headline numbers (Table 1).**
- Germany–Luxembourg: best individual models MLP with FLin 23.12/12.33, MLP with RLin 23.37/12.39 (RMSE/MAE). Ensembles: **MLP (BOA) RMSE 22.21** (best RMSE), **MLP with RLin and OLS (BOA) MAE 11.55, rMAE 31.65%** (best MAE/rMAE); MLP with RLin (BOA) 22.48/11.66/31.98%; BOA all 22.97/11.83. Benchmarks: **DNN 24.97/13.95/38.25%, LEAR 26.57/16.16/44.30%, GAM Online 27.35/15.34/42.05%**.
- Spain: **MLP with RLin (BOA) 14.84/10.79/34.84%** best on all three metrics; DNN 16.93/12.54/40.51%, LEAR 17.77/13.24/42.75%.
- **Headline: 11–12% RMSE and 14–17% MAE reduction vs the better benchmark (DNN)** across the two markets. **BOA ensembling vs the individual counterpart model: 4–11% MAE reduction (DE-LU)** — e.g. MLP 13.07→11.66, MLP with RLin 12.39→11.66, RLin 13.86→12.65. Even the linear FLin beats DNN in Germany. DM tests: MLP with RLin and OLS (BOA) significantly more accurate than everything except MLP (BOA) and MLP with RLin (BOA); benchmarks beaten significantly by most proposed models; LEAR not significantly better than any model.
- Runtime (M1 MacBook, full 730-day study): proposed models 6 s–6 min; **LEAR ≈ 1.5–2 h, DNN ≈ 4.5 h (DE) / 6 h (ES), GAM Online ≈ 9.5 min**. Ablation (Table 2, MLP with RLin, DE): with partial online learning MAE 12.39 / RMSE 23.37 / 39.4 s; non-online Tuned 12.78/278 s; Update-config 12.81/204 s; Initial-config 14.05/16 s — online learning both faster (~5×) and more accurate. Sensitivity: pure online updating (D_up = 1) is bad (MAE 27); larger update windows monotonically improve MAE at higher cost — i.e., "partial" (mini-batch windows) is essential.
- Robustness: alternative 3-year test spanning the 2021–23 energy crisis — MLP with RLin (BOA) MAE pre-crisis/crisis/post-crisis 5.94/24.31/11.51 vs DNN 6.56/29.76/17.73, LEAR 7.88/32.54/17.01: **≈18% MAE / 23% RMSE better than benchmarks during the crisis**. PJM dataset check (test 27-12-2016–24-12-2018): proposed model beats DNN on accuracy and runtime (Appendix Table 5).
- Scope notes: point forecasts only (probabilistic = future work); no forgetting, no weight smoothing across hours or quantiles; per-hour BOA weights treated independently.

## Cross-paper answers to the positioning questions

**BOA vs EWA/ML-Poly — guarantees.** Wintenberger 2017: BOA is the first online procedure optimal *in deviation* for model selection (fast rate log(M)/n); EWA/PRM optimal only in expectation, provably suboptimal in deviation; adaptive multi-learning-rate BOA optimal up to log log n. Berrisch–Ziel 2021: quantile loss is convex but not exp-concave ⇒ EWA has no selection-optimality guarantee for quantile/CRPS learning, whereas gradient fully-adaptive BOA achieves O(log K/t) to the best expert (needs conditional density bounded below) and O(sqrt(log K/t)) to the best convex combination, each up to log log t. Empirically BOA > ML-Poly > EWA in both BZ papers.

**Does smoothing weights help, and across what?** BZ2021: P-spline smoothing **across quantiles** improves pointwise BOA in simulations at all sample sizes and on EUA data (best spec, DM-significant); shrinkage direction should be toward *constant* weights. BZ2024 is the only paper smoothing **across hours**: penalized smoothing across marginals (λ^mv) plus quantiles (λ^pr); best model uses both + forgetting; but **forgetting contributes most of the gain**, and the fitted smoothing is stronger across quantiles than across hours. No paper smooths *served* weights across adjacent hours post hoc the way this project does (±1-hour smoothing of serving weights) — closest analogue is BZ2024's penalized λ^mv smoothing inside the update, and Devaine et al.'s share updates across the 48 half-hours of a delivery day.

**Aggregation vs best single expert — reported gains.** Devaine (load): gradient/fixed-share sleeping-expert rules beat the best single expert by ≈7% (Slovak) to ≈20–24% RMSE (French), and even beat the best-convex *oracle* by 3–12%. Nowotarski–Weron (prices, point): SIMPLE average beats best individual by ≈3% WMAE and all combinations beat all individuals; QRA cuts Winkler scores ≈2–6%. BZ2024 (prices, probabilistic): best BOA combination beats the best single DDNN expert by ≈6.5% CRPS and the uniform average by ≈0.8% (significant). Bridging 2026 (prices, point): per-hour BOA ensembles cut MAE 4–11% vs their own best individual models and 14–17% vs DNN benchmark. Consistent story: biggest gains come from heterogeneous/complementary experts; vs a *uniform average* the online-learning edge is real but modest (sub-1% to a few %) unless experts are very unequal (then huge, cf. EUA where most experts were worse than naive).

**Forgetting.** BZ2021 (eq. 43) and BZ2024 (eq. 25) use exponential forget on R, E, V; optimal rates near 0; kills asymptotic guarantees but is empirically the single most valuable extension in BZ2024. Devaine's fixed-share α plays the analogous tracking role (best α ≈ 0.01–0.2). Wintenberger and the 2026 paper use no forgetting (the 2026 paper's plain fully-adaptive BOA matches this project's forget-factor-1.0 configuration).

**Per-hour treatment.** Devaine: 24 separate per-hour problems (Slovak) or joint 48-half-hour operational scheme (French). NW2016: joint daily estimation, per-hour evaluation of PIs. BZ2024: joint multivariate treatment of 24 hours with cross-hour smoothing. Bridging 2026: independent per-hour BOA weights — the closest published setup to this project's per-hour BOA over 13 members, but without the cross-hour (±1) smoothing of serving weights, which therefore appears to be a genuine delta vs the published EPF literature (only BZ2024's in-update marginal smoothing is comparable).
